## Family Co-Learning Flow

### 1. Warm kid-friendly explanation

Money is a tool people use so they can get what they need without having to swap one exact thing for another exact thing.

Think about a trade with no money at all. You have something. You want something else. The other person has to want *your* thing, and you have to want *their* thing, at the same time. If that match is missing, the trade can get stuck.

Money helps because lots of people will take it. You can give someone money for what they have. Later, they can use that same money to get what *they* want from someone else. You do not have to find a perfect two-person match.

Money does three everyday jobs:

1. **You can pay with it.** People will take it in a trade.
2. **You can measure with it.** Prices let you compare things: "This costs 2, that costs 8."
3. **You can save it for later.** If you don't spend it today, you can often spend that value another day.

Not every object does those jobs well. A sandwich is great for lunch, but it does not keep well, and many people will not take a sandwich as payment for a bike. Useful money is usually:

- **Durable:** it does not spoil or fall apart easily.
- **Portable:** you can carry it.
- **Divisible:** you can split it into smaller amounts (like making change).
- **Uniform:** one piece is like another piece of the same kind. (A grown-up word for this is *fungible*: one unit can stand in for another.)
- **Scarce:** there is not an endless pile of it, so people treat it as valuable.
- **Widely accepted:** many people will take it.

People have used many kinds of money over time, not only coins and bills. Cowrie shells were used in many parts of Asia and Africa. On the Pacific island of Yap, people used large stone disks called rai. Those are just examples of things groups of people have accepted in trade. (The source list at the end of this pack names the pages these examples come from.) We will look more carefully at history in Lesson 2.

> **Stretch (older kids):** Economists give the three jobs formal names. Paying is the *medium of exchange*. Measuring and comparing prices is the *unit of account*. Saving value for later is the *store of value*. Something counts as money when people widely accept it as payment — not just because it is metal, paper, or pretty.

#### Worked example 1 — When lemonade will not buy stickers

All amounts below are **pretend**.

Maya made lemonade. She wants a pack of stickers.

Jordan has a pack of stickers. Jordan does **not** want lemonade. Jordan wants a soccer ball.

Maya and Jordan cannot make a direct trade. Maya has what Jordan does not want, and Jordan has what Maya wants. The match is missing.

Then Sam comes by. Sam wants lemonade and will pay **2 pretend tokens**. Maya sells Sam one cup of lemonade and now has 2 tokens.

The sticker pack also costs **2 pretend tokens**. Maya pays Jordan 2 tokens and gets the stickers.

Jordan still wants a soccer ball, which costs **8 pretend tokens**. Jordan does not have enough yet, but the 2 tokens can be kept and added to later. The tokens did not melt or spoil the way a cup of leftover lemonade would.

**What to notice:**

- The tokens were used to **pay**.
- Everyone could see the **prices** (2, 2, and 8) and compare them.
- Jordan could **save** the 2 tokens toward the ball.
- Check the arithmetic: Maya received 2 and spent 2. She has 0 tokens left and has the stickers. Jordan has 2 tokens, which is 6 short of 8.

### 2. Parent Coach Insight

Read this layer with (or just after) the kid explanation. It gives you the standard toolkit, the second worked example, and one important caution about the usual "first there was barter" story.

#### A working definition

The Federal Reserve's education materials define money as **any good that is widely accepted as final payment for goods and services**. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis uses a matching idea: money is **anything generally accepted in exchange for goods and services**, and it is defined by the jobs it does.

So money is not only "coins and bills." Coins and bills are common forms. What makes something money is that people will take it in payment and use it for the functions below.

#### The three functions of money

Economists commonly describe three functions (Federal Reserve Education; St. Louis Fed; Bank of England):

1. **Medium of exchange.** Money is widely accepted as a method of payment. It stands in the middle of a trade so the seller does not have to want the exact item the buyer happens to have.
2. **Unit of account.** Money is a shared yardstick for measuring and comparing value. It is much easier to say a computer costs a certain number of dollars than to quote the price in t-shirts, bicycles, or bushels of corn (Federal Reserve Education).
3. **Store of value.** People can hold money and spend it later. The Bank of England uses a simple picture: an ice cream worth £2 will melt if you try to save the ice cream itself; a £2 coin can be spent another day. Money is a useful store of value, but **not a perfect one**. Inflation can slowly reduce what a unit of money will buy over time (Federal Reserve Education; St. Louis Fed). That limit matters later, when the sequence asks what makes money more or less reliable.

A house, a collectible, or a box of tools can also store value. Those things are not money unless people also widely accept them as payment and use them to measure prices. The three functions work together.

#### Why people use money: the double coincidence of wants

**Barter** is trading goods or services for other goods or services, without money (St. Louis Fed glossary).

For a two-person barter trade to work, each person must want what the other is offering, at the right time and place. That situation is called the **double coincidence of wants** (St. Louis Fed). The Federal Reserve's education transcript calls a coincidence of wants "the unlikely case that two people each have something that the other wants at the right time and place to make an exchange."

The usual teaching example is a skilled person who needs a repair (a musician who needs a mechanic, or an accountant who needs a car fixed). The repair person may not want a concert, or tax help, today. Searching for a perfect match takes time. Money lowers that search cost: the musician or accountant can be paid in money by people who want their work, then use that money to pay a mechanic who wants money (St. Louis Fed).

This is a **model of why a widely accepted medium of exchange is useful**. It is a good kitchen-table tool. It is not, by itself, a complete history of how money began. See the debate note below.

#### Useful properties (characteristics) of money

Federal Reserve Education lists six characteristics that make some forms of money more useful than others: **durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity, limited supply, and acceptability**. In this lesson we use plain-language labels that match those six:

| Plain label | Official name | What it means in practice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Durable | Durability | It stands up to use and time. Corn can rot; a worn bill can be replaced. |
| Portable | Portability | It is easy to carry. A cow is hard to take to the store; bills fit in a pocket. |
| Divisible | Divisibility | It can be broken into smaller amounts so people can pay many different prices. A $20 bill can be changed into a $10, a $5, four $1 bills, and four quarters. A cow is not easily divided. |
| Uniform / fungible | Uniformity | Units of the same kind are alike in value. Cows differ in size and quality. $20 bills are made to be the same size, shape, and value. *Fungible* is a useful word for "one unit can stand in for another of the same kind." |
| Scarce | Limited supply | If the supply can be increased without limit, each unit tends to become less useful as money. |
| Widely accepted | Acceptability | People will take it as payment. U.S. paper money is also legal tender for "all debts, public and private," which supports acceptance, but everyday use still depends on other people being willing to take it (Federal Reserve Education). |

These are criteria, not a moral ranking of cultures. Cowrie shells were popular in part because they were easy to count (Bank of England). Yap's rai stones could be durable, limited in supply, and accepted, and ownership could change by agreement even when a stone was too large to move; they were much weaker on portability, divisibility, and uniformity (Bank of Canada Museum; St. Louis Fed Open Vault). Cattle have been used as money in some times and places, and they score poorly on several of the same tests (Federal Reserve Education). Lesson 2 returns to these examples in historical order. Lesson 3 will reuse the same criteria when the sequence asks what "sound" and "unsound" money means.

#### A caution: the "barter came first" story is debated

Many explainers introduce money by asking you to imagine a world of spot barter, then show how money solves the double coincidence of wants. That is the path this lesson uses as a **teaching model**, because it makes the medium-of-exchange function clear.

Anthropologists have debated whether real societies actually ran, first, on that kind of all-purpose barter and then "invented money" to escape it. David Graeber, in *Debt: The First 5,000 Years* (2011), argues that the textbook sequence is not well supported by ethnographic evidence. In a 2011 essay, Graeber quotes Cambridge anthropologist Caroline Humphrey (1985): "No example of a barter economy, pure and simple, has ever been described, let alone the emergence from it of money; all available ethnography suggests that there never has been such a thing." [VERIFY: this lesson quotes Humphrey as Graeber quotes her; the 1985 article was not opened independently for this draft.] Graeber also notes that credit, gift, and debt arrangements appear in the record in ways the simple barter-first story leaves out.

**How to use this with kids:** Keep the lemonade-and-stickers problem. It is real. People do get stuck when wants don't match. Just avoid saying, as a settled fact, "the first humans only bartered, and then they invented money." Lesson 2 will treat history more carefully, in a non-ideological way: what people have used, how commodity money and later fiat money work, and what is known versus what is a model.

#### Worked example 2 — Prices you can compare, and value you can keep

All amounts below are **pretend**.

At a family yard sale, prices are marked in pretend dollars:

- A puzzle costs **4**
- A game costs **8**
- A used bike costs **40**
- A snow cone costs **3**

**Unit of account.** How many puzzles equal one game? 8 ÷ 4 = 2. How many games equal one bike? 40 ÷ 8 = 5. How many puzzles equal one bike? 40 ÷ 4 = 10. You can check all three against the same yardstick without inventing a new "puzzle price" for every pair of items.

**Store of value.** If the family spends 3 now on the snow cone and tries to "save the snow cone" for next Saturday, it will melt, and that value is gone (this is the Bank of England's ice-cream idea, with different numbers). If they keep the 3 pretend dollars instead, they can still spend that amount later. **Parent note:** over a long time, rising prices can mean those 3 units buy a little less. That does not make money useless. It means "store of value" is a job money can do well or poorly, which later lessons will test.

**Medium of exchange.** The person selling the game may not want a puzzle. They will likely take pretend dollars, then use those dollars to get what they want.

### 3. Reflection question

**For adults / parents:** Which of money's three jobs did you rely on most this week — paying (medium of exchange), comparing prices (unit of account), or holding value for later (store of value)? Where did you notice it working less well?

### 4. Practical family activity

**Name:** The two-round market  
**Time:** about 15–20 minutes  
**Rule:** Pretend tokens only. No real money, and no real sats.

**Set-up**

1. Cut or draw at least 10 paper tokens per person. They are pretend, and each token counts as 1.
2. Give each person 2–3 household items to "sell" (books, drawings, toys that will be returned at the end). No one has to give up a special item for real.
3. Each seller marks a pretend price: 1, 2, 3, or 4 tokens.

**Round A — no tokens (barter only)**

For five minutes, try to trade items for items only. If someone wants your item but you do not want theirs, the trade cannot happen unless you find another way (a three-person swap counts if you can really arrange it).

**Round B — tokens allowed**

Now use the pretend tokens. Buy and sell at the posted prices. People may save tokens if they wish.

**Talk about**

- In Round A, how many trades actually happened? Where did you get stuck?
- In Round B, was it easier to get what you wanted? Why?
- Which of the three jobs did the tokens do: paying, measuring prices, or saving?
- If the tokens were muffins instead of paper, what would go wrong?

## Appendix

### Learning goals

After this lesson, learners should be able to:

1. State a simple definition of money: something widely accepted as payment, used to trade, to measure prices, and to hold value for later.
2. Explain, with an example, why a trade can fail when wants do not match (the double coincidence of wants), and how money can help.
3. Name useful properties of money (durable, portable, divisible, uniform/fungible, scarce, widely accepted) and use them to judge whether a made-up object would work well as money.

### Practice problems (easy → hard)

All money amounts are **pretend**. Try the problems before opening the answer key.

**1. Easy — Which job?**  
Alex buys an apple for 2 pretend dollars. Which job is money mainly doing in that moment?

- A. Medium of exchange (paying)
- B. Unit of account only, with no payment
- C. Store of value only, with no trade

**2. Easy–medium — Keep it or eat it?**  
At noon, you may choose a sandwich you will not eat until tomorrow, or 3 pretend dollars. You want lunch tomorrow. Which choice is the better *store of value*, and why?

**3. Medium — Use the yardstick**  
A pencil costs 2, an eraser costs 6, and a binder costs 24 (pretend dollars).

- a. How many pencils equal one eraser?
- b. How many erasers equal one binder?
- c. How many pencils equal one binder?

**4. Medium–hard — Who can trade?**  
Ana has apples and wants bread.  
Ben has bread and wants cheese.  
Cara has cheese and wants apples.

- a. Can any *two* people make a direct trade in which both get what they want? Why or why not?
- b. Name the problem this illustrates.
- c. If they all accept pretend tokens, describe one path that gets each person what they want. (You may choose simple prices, such as 1 token per item.)

**5. Hard — Judge two objects**  
Imagine two candidate "moneys" in a small town:

- **Fresh muffins**, baked each morning, that go stale by the next day.
- **Smooth marked stones** that are fairly alike, easy to carry, hard to make more of, and that almost everyone in town already accepts.

For each object, mark **Good**, **Weak**, or **Not enough information** for these properties: durable, portable, divisible, uniform, scarce, widely accepted. Then answer: which object would work better as money, and give at least two reasons tied to the properties.

### Answer key

**1.** A. The 2 pretend dollars are being used as payment, so the main job in that moment is medium of exchange. (The number 2 also uses the unit of account, but the question asks what is mainly happening: a purchase.)

**2.** The 3 pretend dollars are the better store of value. A sandwich can spoil, dry out, or become unsafe to eat by tomorrow. Money is often better at holding value than food is, though it is still not perfect over long periods (Federal Reserve Education uses corn that might rot as the comparison).

**3.**  
- a. 6 ÷ 2 = 3 pencils equal one eraser.  
- b. 24 ÷ 6 = 4 erasers equal one binder.  
- c. 24 ÷ 2 = 12 pencils equal one binder.  
Check: 4 × 3 = 12, so answers a–c fit together.

**4.**  
- a. No. Ana wants bread, but Ben (who has bread) wants cheese, not apples. Ben wants cheese, but Cara (who has cheese) wants apples, not bread. Cara wants apples, but Ana (who has apples) wants bread, not cheese. No pair has a two-way match.  
- b. The double coincidence of wants is missing.  
- c. One path: each item costs 1 token, and Cara starts with 1 pretend token (a parent can hand the group a starter token). Cara buys apples from Ana. Ana uses that token to buy bread from Ben. Ben uses that token to buy cheese from Cara. Ana ends with bread, Ben with cheese, and Cara with apples plus the token again. (Other token paths can work; the key is that tokens let a seller accept payment even when they do not want the buyer's goods.)

**5.** A solid answer looks like this (wording may vary; reasons must match the properties):

| Property | Fresh muffins | Smooth marked stones |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Durable | Weak (stale by the next day) | Good (stones last) |
| Portable | Good (easy to carry) | Good (described as easy to carry) |
| Divisible | Not enough information / mixed (you might split a muffin, but crumbs are messy and stale fast) | Not enough information unless the town agrees on smaller stones or can make change in stones |
| Uniform | Weak or mixed (muffins differ; they also change as they go stale) | Good ("fairly alike") |
| Scarce | Weak or mixed (they can be baked again each morning) | Good ("hard to make more of") |
| Widely accepted | Not enough information (the prompt does not say people accept muffins as payment) | Good ("almost everyone in town already accepts") |

The stones would work better as money. Two strong reasons: they last (durable / store of value) and they are already widely accepted. Extra credit if a learner also notes limited supply and uniformity. Muffins fail first as a store of value because they spoil.

### Three common mistakes (and how to spot them)

1. **Thinking money is only coins and bills.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says, "If there are no dollars, there is no money."  
   *Redirect:* Money is whatever people widely accept as payment. Coins and bills are familiar forms. Shells, stones, and cattle have also been used (Federal Reserve Education; Bank of England). The test is the functions, not the material.

2. **Thinking any two people can always trade if both have 'something.'**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner is surprised that Maya and Jordan could not trade lemonade for stickers.  
   *Redirect:* Both sides have to want what the other is offering. That double match is the double coincidence of wants. Money is useful because the seller can accept money even when they do not want the buyer's goods.

3. **Thinking money never loses buying power, or that anything valuable is automatically money.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says money "always stays worth the same," or calls a favorite toy "money" because it is precious to them.  
   *Redirect:* Money is not a perfect store of value; inflation can reduce what it buys (Federal Reserve Education; St. Louis Fed). And a thing can be valuable to you without being money. It becomes money only when people widely accept it as payment and can use it to measure and hold value.

### Check for understanding (3 questions)

Use these after the activity. Answers are below, for the parent.

**Q1.** In one or two sentences, what is money?  
**Q2.** Why might a person who bakes bread and a person who mends shoes still fail to trade with each other?  
**Q3.** Name three properties that help an object work well as money, and give one object that would be weak on at least one of those properties.

**Answers**

**A1.** A good answer: money is something people widely accept as payment, so they can trade, compare prices, and save value for later. (It does not have to say "coins" or "bills.")

**A2.** A good answer names the missing match: the cobbler may not want bread today, or the baker may not need shoes today. That is the double coincidence of wants. Money would let each person sell to anyone who will pay, then buy from the other later.

**A3.** A good answer names any three of: durable, portable, divisible, uniform/fungible, scarce (limited supply), widely accepted. A weak object should match the property: ice cream or fruit (not durable), a huge stone (not portable), a live cow (hard to divide and not uniform), ordinary leaves (maybe not scarce or durable), a private IOU only one friend will take (not widely accepted).

### Steelman for and against this lesson's framing

**Steelman for.** Starting with functions and properties gives families a shared, testable vocabulary before later lessons in this sequence. The double-coincidence story, used as a model, matches how central-bank education pages explain why a medium of exchange cuts search costs and supports specialization (Federal Reserve Education; St. Louis Fed). Kids can check their thinking with pretend prices and a two-round market. That is a fair, non-ideological foundation: incentives, outcomes, and useful tools.

**Steelman against.** If the lesson is careless, it can sound as if historians have proved a simple sequence: first barter, then money. Anthropologists, including Graeber in *Debt*, argue that credit, gift, and debt systems are a better description of many moneyless or low-money settings, and that a pure barter economy has not been documented. Leading with "money solves barter" can also underplay trust, law, and social agreement, which the Bank of England emphasizes when it explains that money depends on people trusting that others will accept it. A strong parent guide should treat today's model as a model, and let Lesson 2 carry the history — including what is debated.

### Coherence notes (how this leads into Lessons 2–5)

- **Lesson 2 — History of money (barter → commodity → fiat), at a clear, non-ideological level.** Revisit today's examples (shells, metal, stones, paper) in time order. Distinguish commodity money, representative money, and fiat money using the definitions already used in Federal Reserve and Bank of England explainers. Return to the barter-origin debate so the teaching model from Lesson 1 is not mistaken for a full history.
- **Lesson 3 — Sound money vs. unsound money, with simple criteria.** Reuse the three functions and the six properties as the scorecard. Ask how well a money holds value, measures prices, and stays accepted, without turning the lesson into a political argument.
- **Lesson 4 — Why Bitcoin is interesting in this context (one clear lesson, not a sales pitch).** A later lesson will ask how Bitcoin compares on these same functions and properties, as a question to examine, not as a sales pitch.
- **Lesson 5 — Practical family applications (saving, spending, basic value).** Use today's three jobs in daily family choices, still with no real sats or real money-as-prize for kids. Needs, wants, and saving already appear on the Institute's Money & Mindset track; Lesson 5 should complement that track, not copy it.
