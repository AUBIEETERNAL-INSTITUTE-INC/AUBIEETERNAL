# Lesson 4 — Sound vs unsound money

**Money in this lesson:** Pretend only. No real money, and no real sats, for kids.

This pack has two layers in one lesson:

- **Kid layer:** a warm, simple explanation of "sound" and "unsound" money, a four-check scorecard, and one worked example families can act out with pretend notes.

## Family Co-Learning Flow

### 1. Warm kid-friendly explanation

In Lesson 1, we learned that money is something lots of people agree to accept, and that it does three jobs: it helps us **pay**, it helps us **compare** prices, and it helps us **save for later**. Useful money is usually **durable**, **portable**, **divisible**, **uniform**, **scarce**, and **widely accepted**.

In Lesson 3, we followed money through time — swap it, shell it, slip it, trust it — and we left two questions open: **what if the note keeper quietly made 10 more notes?** And **what if there are more receipts than shells?**

Today's question: **when is money sound, and when is it unsound?**

**Sound money** is money that keeps doing its three jobs well — especially the "save for later" job — because people can trust how much of it there is, and the rules around it.

**Unsound money** is money that stops doing those jobs well. That can happen if too much of it is made too quickly, if it is easy to fake, or if it does not last. Then prices can jump, and the same amount in the Later jar buys less than it used to. Sometimes people even stop accepting it.

We are not picking a "best" kind of money. Shells, coins, paper receipts, and today's notes can all be more sound or less sound, depending on how they are used and how their supply is handled.

A simple four-check scorecard (use it on any kind of money):

1. **Does it last?** A cup of leftover lemonade spoils. A button, a coin, or a well-made note lasts longer.
2. **Can it be divided?** Can you make change, or is it one big lump?
3. **Is it hard to fake?** If anyone can make a copy that looks the same, people may stop trusting it.
4. **Can people trust the supply and the rules?** If new money appears much faster than the things people want to buy, each piece can buy less. If the rules keep changing in surprising ways, trust can fall.

Checks 1–3 reuse Lesson 1's ideas of durable, divisible, and uniform money. Check 4 is the big one for this lesson: **scarce** (a limited supply) plus trust in the rules.

#### Worked example 1 — Maya and the extra notes

All amounts below are **pretend**. No real money.

Maya's family is running a tiny market. There are **10 pretend notes** in use, and a cup of lemonade is posted at **1 note**.

Maya has **5 notes** in her Later jar. At today's price, those 5 notes would buy **5 cups** of lemonade (5 ÷ 1 = 5). She does not spend them yet. She is saving.

Then the vault keeper writes **10 more notes** and hands them out, without any more lemonade, stickers, or toy cars arriving. Now there are **20 notes**, but still the same amount of things to buy.

The family posts a new lemonade price: **2 notes** a cup. (This is a pretend simplification, not a law. In real life, prices do not always double just because the number of notes doubles. Here we use a clear doubling so the math is easy to check.)

**What to notice:**

- Maya still has **5 notes**. She did not spend any.
- Those 5 notes now buy only **2 cups**, with **1 note** left over (2 + 2 = 4, and 5 − 4 = 1). That is less than the 5 cups they would have bought before.
- The notes can still be used to **pay**, if people keep accepting them.
- **Comparing** got a little harder for a moment, because the number next to "lemonade" changed.
- The Later jar **shrank in what it could buy**, even though Maya was careful and did not spend.

The problem started when the **supply of notes** changed faster than the things people wanted to buy. That is check 4.

> **Stretch (older kids):** Grown-ups call a general rise in prices **inflation**. The Federal Reserve's education materials note that money is a useful way to save for later, but **not a perfect one**, because inflation can slowly reduce what a unit of money will buy. Ordinary inflation is usually slow. A very fast, runaway rise is sometimes called **hyperinflation**. Many experts use a rule of thumb from economist Phillip Cagan: prices rising more than **50% in one month**. Hyperinflation is rare. The parent layer looks at two real cases families can check themselves, without asking anyone to pick a side about today's money.

### 2. Parent Coach Insight

Read this layer with (or just after) the kid explanation. It gives the standard vocabulary, a scorecard you can reuse, and a few historical cases with dates and sources you can check yourself. This section is education, not advice about what to buy, hold, or invest in. It does not declare gold, paper, or any other form of money the "winner."

#### A working definition, not a team to join

In ordinary speech, **sound money** means money that people can reasonably trust to keep doing money's jobs — especially holding value over time — because its supply and rules are credible. **Unsound money** means money that is failing those tests, often because the supply rose far faster than the goods and services people wanted to buy, or because people lost trust in the rules.

Those are criteria, not a loyalty test. A metal coin can be unsound if it is quietly mixed with cheaper metal, or if it is easy to produce in large amounts. A paper or fiat note can be relatively sound if people trust that its supply and value will stay reasonably stable. The Bank of England's 2014 *Quarterly Bulletin* article "Money in the modern economy: an introduction" says that for modern money to work, people must trust that notes are hard to counterfeit, and must have faith that their value will stay broadly stable — which, the Bank says, generally means keeping inflation low and stable. It adds that since Britain left gold convertibility in 1931, various ways of keeping money's value stable have been tried, "with differing degrees of success."

#### The Federal Reserve's six characteristics, with limited supply at the center

Federal Reserve Education's audio lesson "Functions of Money" lists six characteristics that make some forms of money more useful than others: **durability, portability, divisibility, uniformity, limited supply, and acceptability**. Lesson 1 already used plain-language labels for those six. This lesson leans on two of them especially:

- **Limited supply.** The same lesson says: "In order to maintain its value, money must have a limited supply." In its cattle-versus-$20-bills comparison, it notes that if cows were used as money, ranchers would try to increase the supply, "which would decrease their value." It also notes that money is not a perfect store of value, because inflation slowly erodes purchasing power.
- **Acceptability and uniformity.** People must be willing to take the money, and units of the same kind should stand in for one another. The Bank of England adds a closely related trust test: notes should be hard to counterfeit.

The kid-layer scorecard maps onto this toolkit:

| Kid check | Grown-up words |
|---|---|
| Does it last? | Durability |
| Can it be divided? | Divisibility (and, in a family market, portability) |
| Is it hard to fake? | Uniformity, plus the Bank of England's "hard to counterfeit" trust test |
| Can people trust the supply and the rules? | Limited supply, acceptability, and credible rules |

The other characteristics still matter. A form of money can pass the supply test and still be a poor everyday tool if it is too heavy, too uneven, or easy to spoil. Lesson 3's corn-versus-coins example was about durability. This lesson's "extra notes" example is about supply and trust.

#### What "too much, too fast" looks like in history

The worked example is pretend. The pattern it is teaching — when the supply of money races ahead, money can stop doing its jobs well — shows up in real cases. The three below are chosen because families can check the sources themselves. They are not a claim that every paper currency, or every government, ends this way. Ordinary inflation is usually much slower than these episodes.

**1. Roman silver coins mixed with cheaper metal (debasement).**  
Over time, Roman authorities reduced the amount of precious metal in silver coins by mixing in cheaper metal, a process historians call **debasement**. Researchers at the University of Warwick and the University of Liverpool have documented this in scientific analyses of the silver *denarius* (University of Warwick press release, 6 April 2022). A later University of Warwick study notes that debasement of silver Roman coinage is a well-known phenomenon in the first three centuries CE, and that mints often used surface enrichment so a coin could look like pure silver on the outside even when the inside was a silver–copper alloy (Hampshire, Butcher and others, *Heritage*, 2019). Because a coin's surface can look more silver than its core, this lesson does **not** use a percentage table. The teaching point is the **direction**: many Roman "silver" coins contained less silver than they once had. Mixing cheaper metal into a coin is one way to increase the supply of coins without adding the same amount of precious metal — a real-world cousin of "making more notes."

**2. Germany, 1923 (Weimar hyperinflation).**  
Deutsche Bundesbank, in "Inflation – lessons learnt from history," describes the months of hyperinflation in 1923: the value of the German currency fell so fast that in many places wages were paid daily; people collected notes in bags and suitcases and crowded into shops to exchange the money for goods as quickly as possible; traders kept raising prices; and some would sell only for food and coal, or closed altogether. The same essay traces a longer path: in 1914, German banknotes were no longer redeemable in gold and could be covered by government bonds instead; the government borrowed increasingly from the Reichsbank, which printed more and more banknotes; after 1918, war debt, social spending, and reparation demands added pressure; more money went into circulation without a matching growth in the supply of goods; and huge price increases followed. By 1923, the Bundesbank writes, the currency "could no longer function as a general means of payment or store of value." In November 1923 the Mark was replaced by the Rentenmark. Inflation had almost completely devalued financial assets and liabilities denominated in Mark. The Bundesbank adds a stark official comparison: German war debt valued at **154 billion Mark** dropped to just **15.4 Pfennig** on the day the Rentenmark was introduced. A later Bundesbank page on historical purchasing power states that, under the Coinage Act of 30 August 1924, the official rate was **1 Reichsmark = 1 trillion Mark**. The arithmetic of that rate matches the war-debt comparison (see Worked example 2). This is a picture of money failing all three jobs at once, especially saving for later. It is not a claim about any currency in use today.

**3. Zimbabwe, 2007–09.**  
Encyclopaedia Britannica's article "hyperinflation" lists Zimbabwe (2007–09) among the famous modern cases, and notes that Zimbabwe abandoned its currency in 2009 and adopted a multicurrency system. Official inflation statistics do not give a single clean peak for the final months. Steve H. Hanke and Alex K. F. Kwok, in the *Cato Journal* (Spring/Summer 2009), write that the government terminated official inflation reporting before the last explosive months of the episode, and that the last official 2008 inflation figures were for July (released in October). On 16 January 2009, *The Guardian* reported that Zimbabwe had unveiled a **100 trillion** Zimbabwe dollar banknote, and that the Reserve Bank was also introducing 10 trillion, 20 trillion, and 50 trillion notes, in a move "to keep pace with hyper-inflation." The paper, quoting state media, said the new family of notes would come into circulation gradually, starting with the 10 trillion note. A BBC News report on 12 June 2015, when the central bank was formalizing the end of the local dollar, said foreign currencies such as the U.S. dollar and the South African rand had been used for most transactions **since 2009**, that the highest denomination had been a **$100 trillion** Zimbabwean dollar note, and that officials had given up on reporting official inflation statistics ahead of the abandonment of the Zimbabwean dollar in January 2009. Because official reporting stopped, and later estimates of a peak rate differ, this lesson does **not** use any one peak-inflation percentage as a teaching number. The teaching point is the one the kid layer can use: when notes get larger and larger, very quickly, money is struggling to be a measuring stick or a way to save for later.

Causes of these episodes are debated, and this pack does not take a political side. The shared monetary fact is simpler: a rapidly expanding supply, plus a collapse of trust, can stop money from doing its jobs.

#### The gold standard was one set of rules, not the only possible scorecard

A few dated turning points are easy to check. The Bank of England's 2014 article "Money in the modern economy: an introduction" says the Bank "permanently abandoned offering gold in return for notes in 1931 so that Britain could better manage its economy during the Great Depression," and that since then Bank of England money has been fiat money. In the United States, Federal Reserve History records that President Franklin D. Roosevelt signed the Gold Reserve Act on **30 January 1934**. The Act transferred ownership of all monetary gold in the United States to the U.S. Treasury and prohibited the Treasury and financial institutions from redeeming dollars for gold. People and institutions received currency at **$35 per ounce**, which "reduced the gold value of the dollar to 59 percent of the value set by the Gold Act of 1900, which equaled $20.67 per ounce." On the evening of **15 August 1971**, President Richard Nixon announced that the gold window would be closed: foreign governments could no longer exchange their dollars for gold (Federal Reserve History, "Nixon Ends Convertibility of U.S. Dollars to Gold and Announces Wage/Price Controls"). A gold (or other metal) standard is one way societies have tried to limit the supply of money and make the rules easier to see: in principle, you cannot issue more notes than the metal promise will support. It also has costs, which the Bank of England notes: commodities used as money have other uses (construction, jewellery), "there is a cost to using them as money," and Britain left gold convertibility in 1931 to manage the economy during the Depression. Economists still debate those choices. This lesson does not settle the debate. It only offers the same scorecard for metal, receipts, and fiat money: does it last, can it be divided, is it hard to fake, and can people trust the supply and the rules?

#### What the history teaches about incentives, outcomes, and sovereignty

Without endorsing any system, three patterns show up:

- **Incentives:** a vault keeper, a mint, or an issuer who can create more money without a matching increase in goods faces a temptation. The kid-layer "extra notes" game makes that incentive visible without blaming any living person.
- **Outcomes depend on supply and trust.** The Federal Reserve's education materials say money must have a limited supply in order to maintain its value. The Bank of England says people must have faith that value will stay broadly stable. When those conditions fail badly, as the Bundesbank describes in 1923, money can stop working as a means of payment or a store of value.
- **Sovereignty, in the family sense:** a family that can use a simple scorecard is better able to ask good questions about the money it uses. That is the point of this lesson. Lesson 5 will apply the same scorecard, carefully and without a sales pitch, to a newer form of money some people find interesting. Lesson 6 turns to everyday family uses.

#### Worked example 2 — Reading the 1924 "1 trillion" rate

All of the official figures here come from Deutsche Bundesbank; the arithmetic is ours.

- Under the Coinage Act of 30 August 1924, **1 Reichsmark = 1 trillion Mark**.  
  1 trillion, in the short scale used in these official English pages, is **1,000,000,000,000**.
- The Bundesbank also reports that war debt valued at **154 billion Mark** dropped to **15.4 Pfennig** on the day the Rentenmark was introduced.
- 154 billion is 154,000,000,000.  
  154,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000,000,000 = **0.154** Mark.
- 1 Mark = 100 Pfennig, so 0.154 Mark = **15.4 Pfennig** — matching the Bundesbank's comparison.

**What to notice:** a change this large is not "prices went up a little." It is what it looks like when money has almost completely lost its ability to save value for later. The people who still held Mark savings were holding a measuring stick that had been shrunk by a trillion-to-one rule. That is why the kid layer's Later jar is the right picture, in a much gentler form.

### 3. Reflection question

**For kids:** Look at a button, a scrap of paper, and a cup of water. If your family had to use one of them as pretend money for a week, which would you pick, and why? Use the four checks: does it last, can it be divided, is it hard to fake, and can people trust the supply?

**For adults / parents:** Why do *you* trust the money you are paid in to buy roughly what you expect next month or next year? Is it the object itself, the law, the fact that everyone else accepts it, the rules that limit its supply, or something else? What would make you trust the supply and the rules more, or less?

### 4. Practical family activity

**Name:** Sound or Unsound  
**Time:** about 20–25 minutes  
**Rule:** Pretend items only. No real money, and no real sats.

**Set-up**

1. Gather four everyday items to score (for example: a button, a leftover snack, a scrap of paper, and a coin-shaped piece of cardboard).
2. Cut about **20** small paper slips for pretend notes, and put **10** of them into play. Keep the other 10 in a bowl, out of sight, as the "vault."
3. Set out a few "shop" items with posted prices in notes (keep the arithmetic small: 1, 2, or 3 notes).
4. Give each person a few notes, and give one person a **Later jar** with 4 notes that they are not allowed to spend in the first round.

**How to play (three short rounds)**

1. **Score it:** as a family, run each of the four everyday items through the four checks. Which would make the most sound pretend money for a week, and which would be unsound? There is no prize for a "right" favorite — the point is to use the checks.
2. **Trade it:** buy and sell with the 10 notes that are in play. At the end of the round, count what the Later jar could still buy at the posted prices.
3. **Add more notes:** an adult (the vault keeper) now puts the extra 10 notes into play, without adding more shop items. The family may change posted prices if they want. Trade again. Count what the Later jar can buy now.

**At the end, talk about**

- Which of the four checks did the leftover snack fail? Which might the scrap of paper fail if anyone can copy it?
- After the extra notes appeared, what happened to prices? To the Later jar?
- How is round 3 like Lesson 3's question, "What if the note keeper made 10 more notes?"
- Which of money's three jobs from Lesson 1 (paying, comparing, saving for later) felt hardest after the extra notes?

## Appendix

### Learning goals

After this lesson, learners should be able to:

1. Explain "sound money" and "unsound money" in plain words: sound money keeps doing money's three jobs well, especially saving for later, because people can trust the supply and the rules; unsound money is failing those tests.
2. Use a four-check scorecard — does it last, can it be divided, is it hard to fake, can people trust the supply and the rules — on more than one form of money, without declaring a winner.
3. Show, with pretend numbers, how making more notes without more goods can shrink what a Later jar can buy, and name one historical case (with a date) where money lost value very quickly.

### Practice problems (easy → hard)

All amounts are **pretend** unless a historical source is named.

**1. Easy — Use the scorecard**  
For each item, say which of the four checks it is weakest on, and why:

- a. A leftover sandwich used as pretend money  
- b. A giant stone that is hard to move or split  
- c. A paper slip anyone in the room can copy with a pencil  
- d. A limited set of 10 family buttons, counted out in the open, that everyone agrees to accept

**2. Easy–medium — The Later jar**  
A Later jar holds 8 pretend notes. Lemonade costs 2 notes a cup.

- a. How many cups can the jar buy now?  
- b. The vault keeper adds more notes, and the family posts a new price of 4 notes a cup. The jar still holds 8 notes. How many cups can it buy now?  
- c. Did the saver spend any notes? What changed?

**3. Medium — Which check failed?**  
In Maya's market there were 10 notes and lemonade cost 1 note. Then there were 20 notes, still the same lemonade, and the posted price became 2 notes. Which scorecard check is this mostly about? In one sentence, say why the notes can still be used to pay even while the Later jar buys less.

**4. Medium–hard — The 1924 rate**  
Deutsche Bundesbank states that, under the Coinage Act of 30 August 1924, 1 Reichsmark = 1 trillion Mark, and that war debt valued at 154 billion Mark dropped to 15.4 Pfennig on the day the Rentenmark was introduced.

- a. Write 1 trillion as a 1 followed by zeros.  
- b. Compute 154,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000,000,000.  
- c. Convert your answer into Pfennig (1 Mark = 100 Pfennig). Does it match 15.4?  
- d. In one or two sentences, what does this say about money's "save for later" job in that episode?

**5. Hard — Not the same as "paper versus metal"**  
A family says, "Metal coins are always sound, and paper notes are always unsound."

- a. Give one reason, from this lesson, that a metal coin might be unsound.  
- b. Give one reason that a paper or fiat note might still do money's jobs reasonably well.  
- c. What question would you ask next, instead of picking a winner?

### Answer key

**1.**  
- a. **Does it last?** A sandwich spoils, so it is a weak way to save for later (the same idea as Lesson 3's corn).  
- b. **Can it be divided?** (It is also weak on being easy to carry, which is Lesson 1's "portable.")  
- c. **Is it hard to fake?** If anyone can copy it, trust can fall.  
- d. Stronger on **trust the supply and the rules** (a counted, limited set) and on **accepting it** if the family agrees. It may still be only fairly durable or divisible, depending on the buttons.

**2.**  
- a. 8 ÷ 2 = **4 cups**.  
- b. 8 ÷ 4 = **2 cups**.  
- c. No. The saver did not spend. What changed was the **price**, after more notes were added, so the same 8 notes buy less.

**3.** Mostly check 4: **can people trust the supply and the rules?** The supply of notes rose faster than the lemonade. The notes can still be used to pay if people keep accepting them; what got weaker is how much each note buys, and how well the Later jar saves.

**4.**  
- a. **1,000,000,000,000** (a 1 and 12 zeros).  
- b. 154,000,000,000 ÷ 1,000,000,000,000 = **0.154**.  
- c. 0.154 × 100 = **15.4 Pfennig**, which matches the Bundesbank figure.  
- d. A good answer: money that was being saved in Mark almost completely lost its ability to hold value; a huge debt, and huge savings, were shrunk by a trillion-to-one change.

**5.**  
- a. A metal coin can be **debased** (mixed with cheaper metal), or it can be produced in larger amounts, so "metal" is not automatically sound.  
- b. A paper or fiat note can still work if people trust it, if it is hard to fake, and if its supply and rules keep its value reasonably stable (Federal Reserve Education; Bank of England).  
- c. A good next question: "Let's run it through the four checks," or "Can people trust the supply and the rules?"

### Three common mistakes (and how to spot them)

1. **Thinking "sound" means "the kind I already like."**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says gold is always good, or paper is always bad, before using the checks.  
   *Redirect:* sound and unsound are test results, not team names. Run the four checks on each form.
2. **Thinking any price rise means money is collapsing.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner treats ordinary, slow inflation as the same thing as 1923 or 2008.  
   *Redirect:* the Federal Reserve notes that money is not a perfect store of value, because inflation can slowly erode purchasing power. Hyperinflation is rare and much faster. Use the word carefully.
3. **Counting the notes in the Later jar and stopping there.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says "Maya still has 5 notes, so nothing changed."  
   *Redirect:* ask what those 5 notes can *buy* before and after the new price. The job is saving value, not saving a pile of paper.

### Check for understanding (3 questions)

**Q1.** In your own words, what is the difference between sound money and unsound money?  
**Q2.** Maya has 5 notes. Lemonade goes from 1 note a cup to 2 notes a cup. What can her Later jar buy after the change, and which scorecard check is this mostly about?  
**Q3.** Why is "paper versus metal" not a good enough test for this lesson?

**Answers**

**A1.** Sound money keeps doing money's three jobs well, especially saving for later, because people can trust how much of it there is and the rules around it. Unsound money is failing those tests — often because too much was made too fast, it is easy to fake, or it does not last.

**A2.** The 5 notes now buy **2 cups**, with **1 note** left over. This is mostly check 4: trusting the supply and the rules.

**A3.** Because a metal coin can be mixed with cheaper metal or made in larger amounts, and a paper or fiat note can still work when people trust its supply, its rules, and that it is hard to fake. The four checks work on every form.

### Steelman for and against this lesson's framing

**Steelman for.** A four-check scorecard gives younger learners a tool they can actually use, and it reuses Lesson 1's characteristics and Lesson 3's open question about extra notes. It stays 501(c)(3)-safe: no investment advice, no sales pitch, and no claim that one living currency is "the" sound money. The parent layer adds checkable sources (Federal Reserve Education, the Bank of England, Deutsche Bundesbank, contemporaneous reporting on Zimbabwe, and a cautious reading of Roman debasement) and distinguishes ordinary inflation from rare hyperinflation. That is a fair foundation for Lesson 5, which applies the same scorecard to a newer form of money, and for Lesson 6's family uses.

**Steelman against.** "Sound money" is a loaded phrase in adult politics. Some families will hear it as a slogan for gold or against today's money, even if the lesson says it is a scorecard. A short pack also cannot do full justice to why governments expand the money supply, how modern central banks try to keep inflation low and stable, or the long scholarly debate on Roman fineness figures. The 1923 and 2008–09 cases are extreme and can scare younger learners if the adult layer is read too early. A strong parent guide keeps the kid example in pretend notes, treats the historical cases as rare warnings rather than everyday life, and leaves any judgment about today's money to the checks — not to a slogan.

- **Lesson 1 — What money is.** Same three jobs and the same six characteristics. This lesson turns "scarce / limited supply" and "save for later" into a scorecard for sound versus unsound.
- **Lesson 2 — Cash flow.** The Later jar is the same picture as Lesson 2's jar and bathtub: money coming in, money going out, and something left for later. This lesson asks a new question: will what is left in the jar still buy as much?
- **Lesson 3 — History of money.** This lesson answers Lesson 3's open questions: "What if the note keeper made 10 more notes?" and "What if there are more receipts than shells?" It applies the same scorecard to barter, commodity money, representative money, and fiat money, without declaring a winner.
- **Lesson 5 — Why Bitcoin is interesting.** Not covered here beyond one sentence of direction: Lesson 5 will apply this same scorecard, as a question to examine, not as a sales pitch. This pack gives no Bitcoin content and no investment advice.

**Teaching simplifications (not historical claims)**

- The worked-example price change (1 note to 2 notes when the note supply doubles) is a pretend simplification, not a claim that prices always double when the money supply doubles.
- Practice-problem amounts (notes, cups, buttons) are invented teaching numbers, except the Bundesbank 1 trillion / 154 billion / 15.4 Pfennig figures, whose arithmetic is shown in Worked example 2.
- Roman debasement is taught as a direction of change, not as a table of exact percentages, because surface enrichment can make a coin look more silver than its core.
- Zimbabwe is taught with the 100 trillion note and the 2009 shift to other currencies, not with a single peak-inflation percentage, because official reporting had stopped and later estimates differ.
- There is no Bitcoin content and no investment advice in this pack; Bitcoin is named only as Lesson 5's topic.
