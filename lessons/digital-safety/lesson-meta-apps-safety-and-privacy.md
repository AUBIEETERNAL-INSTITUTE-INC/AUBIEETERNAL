## Family Co-Learning Flow

### 1. Warm plain-English overview for families

These four apps can be a real gift for a family. They help you share photos, plan a picnic, keep a group chat going, and stay close to people you love. The same tools can also let a stranger reach you, show more of your life than you meant to show, or try to trick you into giving away a password or money.

You do not need to be a technology expert. You need a few steady habits, and a short checkup you can repeat. Think of it like locking the house and agreeing on house rules, not like a one-time test.

**The four habits that work across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger:**

1. **Lock the Door.** Turn on the extra sign-in step (Facebook and Instagram call this two-factor authentication; WhatsApp calls it two-step verification). Keep recovery codes, a recovery email, or a Messenger PIN in a safe place you can find later. Do not tap "trust this device" or "save this browser" on a shared or public device.
2. **Check Who Can See and Reach You.** Decide who can see your posts, profile details, and photos, and who can message you, add you to a group, or call you. Each app has a place to review this. The defaults are not always the most private option.
3. **Pause Before Clicking or Sharing.** If a message rushes you, asks for a password, a PIN, or money, or tells you to move the conversation off the app, stop. Official Help Center pages for Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp all say the same first move: slow down, do not click a suspicious link, and do not send money or personal details until you can check the story another way.
4. **Keep Family Ground Rules Open.** Write a short list you can actually keep: who may accept a new follow or friend request, what to do with a message from an unknown number, and when you will walk through settings again. Rules work better when the family helped write them.

A private account, a locked chat, or end-to-end encryption is a strong lock on the door. It does not decide who you let in. A scam can still arrive from a person, a fake account, or even a friend's account that was taken over. The habits above still apply.

### 2. App-by-app practical safety and effectiveness guides

Use each app for what it does well, then check a short list of settings. If a menu looks different on your phone or computer, open the official Help Center page in Section 6 for that app and follow the steps for your device.

#### Facebook — staying in touch with family and community

**How families can use it well.** Facebook is often where family news, community groups, event plans, and photo albums live. Share on purpose: choose an audience you are comfortable with, and remember that a post can travel farther than the dinner table. A community group is useful when the family's goal is local news or a club update, not when a stranger's link asks you to "confirm your account."

**Settings to check (about 4 or 5):**

1. **Privacy Checkup.** Facebook's Help Center says this guided tool lets you review privacy and security settings in one place. It walks you through who sees what you share, account security, how people find you, data settings, and ad preferences. Some of these settings can be managed only from your main Facebook profile (the one created when you signed up), so switch to that profile if you use more than one. On an iPhone, Facebook's Help Center says: tap **Menu**, then **Settings & privacy**, then **Settings**, then **Privacy Checkup**, then the topic you want to review (for example, **Who can see what you share**). Facebook also publishes steps for a computer, Android, and other devices; the menu may look a little different, so follow the on-screen labels for your device. If you do not see Privacy Checkup, Facebook says to update the app and switch to your main profile.
2. **Who can see what you share.** In Privacy Checkup, this section helps you choose who can see certain profile information (such as your phone number, email, birthday, and relationship status), control who can see your future and past posts, and review who you have blocked.
3. **How people can find you.** In the same checkup, you can choose who can send you friend requests and who can look you up by your phone number or email address.
4. **Two-factor authentication (and login alerts).** Two-factor authentication asks for a special login code or a confirmation when someone tries to open your Facebook account from a browser or device Facebook does not recognize. Facebook's Help Center says you may see either **Accounts Center** or **Meta Account** settings as the place to manage this. On a computer, the documented path is: profile picture (top right) > **Settings and privacy** > **Settings** > **Accounts Center** > **Password and security** > **Two-factor authentication**, then the account you want to update. You can use a security key, login codes from an authentication app, or text-message (SMS) codes. Facebook also lets you create recovery login codes (it describes 10 codes) for times you cannot use your phone. Privacy Checkup can also help you turn on login alerts so you are notified about a login from a place Facebook does not recognize. Do not choose **Save this browser** on a public computer.
5. **A note on stronger protection.** Some Meta accounts are asked to turn on **Advanced Protection**, which can require a passkey, two-factor authentication, or a stronger password. Meta's Help Center says Meta will never ask you for your password in an email or message. If you get an email about Advanced Protection, Meta says you can confirm it came from `security@facebookmail.com`. When in doubt, do not click a link in the message; open Facebook yourself and look in settings.

**Two scams and gotchas to watch for:**

- **"There's a problem with your account — click this link."** Facebook's official scam guidance says scammers often send a phishing email that claims to come from Meta, warns that something is wrong with your account, and asks you to click a link right away. If you get a suspicious email or message claiming to be from Facebook, Facebook says: do not click any links or attachments. Check in your Facebook settings whether it actually came from Facebook. Slow down, spot-check the story, and do not send a password, Social Security number, or credit-card numbers.
- **A "friend" or family member in an emergency, or a fake profile.** Facebook warns that scammers may create fake accounts, or take over a real account, and then ask for money or personal information. Look out for people you do not know (or famous people) asking for money, a "friend" or relative in an emergency, a person who wants to move the conversation off Facebook, or a romance that turns quickly into a request for money. Facebook's advice is simple: if you think it is a scam, do not respond, and report it.

#### Instagram — sharing photos and following interests, with tighter controls

**How families can use it well.** Instagram is useful for photos, short videos, and following hobbies, clubs, or creators the family already trusts. A private account is a practical default for many families: people must be approved as followers before they can see what you share. For teens 13–17, Instagram Teen Accounts already start with stronger built-in limits.

**Settings to check (about 4 or 5):**

1. **Private account.** Instagram's Help Center says that if you are under 18, your account is private by default. Teens under 16 need a parent's or guardian's permission to switch the account to public. Teens 16–17 can change the account to public without permission, unless supervision is set up. Adults can also choose a private account. On Instagram.com, the documented path is: **More** (bottom left) > **Settings** > **Account Privacy** (under **Who can see your content**) > turn on **Private account** > **Switch to private** to confirm. Instagram notes that business profiles cannot be made private; you would first switch back to a personal account.
2. **Message controls.** You can choose whether message requests from people on Instagram go to your Message Requests folder, or whether you receive them at all. Instagram's Help Center path is: **More** > **Settings** > **Messages and story replies** (under **How others can interact with you**) > **Message controls**. Messages from people you follow appear in your Chats list. You need to accept a request before it appears in Chats. In the same **Message controls** screen, you can also choose who can add you to groups: **Everyone on Instagram**, or **Only people you follow on Instagram**.
3. **Hidden Words.** This tool hides comments and message requests you do not want to see, including a custom list of words, phrases, numbers, or emojis. On Instagram.com, go to **More** > **Settings** > **Hidden Words** (under **How others can interact with you**). Instagram describes two automatic filters: **Hide unwanted comments** (on by default) and **Hide unwanted message requests** (off by default on ordinary accounts, but Teen Accounts are automatically set to the strictest Hidden Words setting). Hidden comments still count in the total, and the person who sent a hidden comment or message is not told that it was hidden. The message-request side of Hidden Words is not currently supported on web browsers or Meta Quest, so use the Instagram app when you can. You can also choose who can comment on your posts under **Settings** > **Comments**.
4. **Two-factor authentication.** Instagram's Help Center (the article is now titled "Securing your Meta Account with two-factor authentication") says this protects your account by requiring a code if there is a login attempt from a device Instagram does not recognize. You may see either **Accounts Center** or **Meta Account** settings. Documented path: **More** > **Settings** > **See more in Accounts Center** > **Password and security** > **Two-factor authentication**, then select an account. Methods are: an authentication app (recommended; this method can only be turned on in the Instagram app for Android and iPhone), a text-message code, or WhatsApp (you must turn on the text-message method first). After it is on, you can see login requests, remove trusted devices, and use a backup code if you cannot get a login code. Do not tap **Trust this device** on a public or shared device.
5. **Teen Account built-in limits (for ages 13–17).** If the account belongs to a teen, confirm it is a Teen Account and leave the protective defaults in place unless a parent and teen have a clear reason to change them. Official defaults include a private account (with the permission rules above), messages only from accounts the teen follows or has already connected with, tags and mentions limited to people they follow, Hidden Words set to the strictest level, sleep mode from 10 PM to 7 AM, and a reminder to close Instagram after 60 minutes in a day. Teens under 16 need a parent's or guardian's permission to make these settings less protective. Teens 16–17 can change many of them themselves, unless the account is supervised.

**Two scams and gotchas to watch for:**

- **A message that claims to be from "Instagram security."** Instagram's official scam page says: Instagram will never send you direct messages about your account in the Instagram app. If you get a suspicious email or message claiming to be from Instagram, do not click links or attachments. You can view official Instagram emails from the last 14 days in your Instagram Settings. Watch for anyone claiming to be from Instagram security and asking for your username or password, or offering "verification" services.
- **A stranger who wants to move the chat off Instagram, or a deal that is too good to be true.** Instagram tells people to look out for a request to move the conversation off Instagram, famous people or strangers asking for money, a sudden romance that turns into a request for money, or investment, job, lottery, and shopping offers that rush you. If you think it is a scam, do not respond, and report it.

#### WhatsApp — everyday family and community messages

**How families can use it well.** WhatsApp is often the family's daily thread: "I'm on my way," a photo of the garden, a group for cousins or a neighborhood watch. Personal chats and calls are end-to-end encrypted by default, which WhatsApp says means no one outside the chat, not even WhatsApp, can read or listen to them. That is a strong privacy feature. It does not tell you whether the person on the other end is who they claim to be, and chats with some businesses work differently.

**Settings to check (about 4 or 5):**

1. **Privacy Checkup.** WhatsApp's Privacy Checkup is a step-by-step guide in one place. WhatsApp's Help Center path is: **Settings** > **Privacy** > **Privacy Checkup**, then each section. In it you can: choose who can contact you (including who can add you to groups, Silence unknown callers, and blocked contacts); control personal info (profile photo, last seen and online, read receipts); add more privacy to chats (a fingerprint lock to open chats, a default message timer, and end-to-end encrypted backups); and add more account protection (two-step verification, a passkey, or a recovery email).
2. **Two-step verification.** This optional feature adds another sign-in step to your WhatsApp account. WhatsApp is upgrading the older 6-digit PIN to a password; if you already have a PIN, WhatsApp's Help Center says you can update it to a password. Add a recovery email so you can get back in if you need to reset this. You can also add a passkey to use your face, fingerprint, or screen lock.
3. **Silence unknown callers, and who can add you to groups.** Anyone who has your phone number can call you on WhatsApp. To silence unknown callers: **Settings** > **Privacy** > **Calls**, then turn on **Silence unknown callers**. Silenced calls still appear in the **Calls** tab and in notifications, but the phone will not ring. If you call or message that person, WhatsApp says you will not be able to silence their calls in the future. For groups: by default, WhatsApp sets this to **Everyone**, so even people who are not in your contacts can add you. You can change it to **My Contacts** or **My Contacts Except…**. A change applies to new groups, not groups you are already in. A group invite link is different: your setting does not stop someone from sending you a link, and joining through a link is always your choice. These group-privacy changes are made on your phone, then they sync to WhatsApp Web and Desktop.
4. **Disappearing messages (optional).** You can set messages to disappear after 24 hours, 7 days, or 90 days. This applies to new messages in that chat, not older ones. You can set a default for new individual chats at **Settings** > **Privacy** > **Default message timer**. This is a privacy tool, not a complete "this never existed" tool. WhatsApp notes, for example, that a disappearing message forwarded to a chat with disappearing messages turned off will not disappear there, and that a message can be included in a backup taken before it disappears (it is deleted when that backup is later restored). Someone can also keep a message in some cases.
5. **Chat lock.** You can lock selected chats so they are hidden in a folder behind an extra sign-in step (WhatsApp may label this **Chat lock** or **Chat block**, depending on language). Other people in the chat are not notified. Notifications hide the contact name and content. Calls from contacts in locked chats are not blocked. If you hide locked chats and later forget the secret code, WhatsApp's "unlock and clear" option returns those chats to your chat list but also clears the messages and media in them, so write the code down in a safe place.

**Two scams and gotchas to watch for:**

- **An unknown number that rushes you for money, a password, or a PIN.** WhatsApp's official scam page says to pause and think: Is it an unknown number? Are they rushing you, threatening you, or asking you to trust them? Are they asking you to transfer money or share a password, PIN, or personal information? If you cannot verify who it is, stop the conversation, do not share those details, then block and report the user. You can also turn on **Scam Alert** in WhatsApp Settings; WhatsApp says your messages stay end-to-end encrypted when this optional warning is on.
- **Being added to a group you did not ask to join.** Because the default group setting is **Everyone**, a number that has your phone number may be able to add you. You can leave the group, tighten who can add you, and use **My Contacts Except…** or blocking if the same person keeps adding you. Personal messages stay encrypted, but a group you never chose is still a window into your number and your attention.

#### Messenger — private chats connected to Facebook

**How families can use it well.** Messenger is handy for a quick family chat, a video call, or a thread that started from a Facebook friend or group. You can decide who reaches your main Chats list and who stays in Message requests. Treat a new request as a knock at the door, not as a conversation you have already accepted.

**Settings to check (about 4 or 5):**

1. **Message delivery (who can message you).** On a phone or tablet, Messenger's Help Center path is: open Messenger > **Menu** > **Settings** > **Privacy & safety** > **Message delivery**. You can choose, for each category of people, to deliver messages to your Chats list, to your message requests folder, or **Don't receive requests**. Categories described by Messenger include friends of your friends, people who have your phone number in their contacts (they do not need to be Facebook friends), friends using Messenger without Facebook, and everyone else on Facebook and Messenger. Facebook friends, and some people you have already connected with (for example in Marketplace or with a business you already messaged), can still reach your Chats list as described on that page. New group-chat requests will not be delivered to your Chats list. This control is documented for the mobile apps, not for Messenger on a computer.
2. **End-to-end encryption, and what "End-to-end encrypted" means.** Messenger's Help Center says end-to-end encryption means only you and the people you are communicating with can see or listen to the messages and calls, and no one else, not even Meta, can do so. Meta is in the process of securing personal messages with end-to-end encryption by default. To check a chat, look for **End-to-end encrypted** in the chat settings. When you log in on a new device, that encrypted chat history does not appear automatically; you need to restore it.
3. **Message storage PIN (and a backup plan).** Your end-to-end encrypted chat history is stored in your Messenger backup. The PIN you create is what you use later to restore that history on a new device. Messenger's Help Center says this feature is being introduced gradually and may not be on every account yet. To create a PIN in the Messenger mobile app: **Menu** > **Settings** > **Privacy & safety** > **End-to-end encrypted chats** > **Message storage** > **PIN** > **Create PIN**, then enter it twice. Write it down in a safe place. Messenger cannot reset your PIN or restore your backup for you. If you forget the PIN, you must reset it on a device where you created it or previously entered it (same menu, then **Reset PIN**). If you have a mobile device where you previously used Messenger, a one-time code is another official option.
4. **Security alerts.** These notifications tell you about logins on a new device and about "key changes." Messenger is clear that not every key change means something is wrong: a new key can appear when someone reinstalls Messenger, uninstalls the app, resets their phone, clears app data, or turns automatic backup uploads on or off. To turn on and manage alerts on a mobile device: **Menu** > **Settings** > **Privacy & safety** > **End-to-end encrypted chats** > **Security alerts** > **Manage security alerts**, then use the toggle for each alert you want.
5. **Blocking and reporting.** Messenger's Help Center has tools to block a profile, report a message or conversation, and report someone pretending to be you or someone else. If you see something you think is a scam, Messenger says you should avoid responding and report the conversation.

**Two scams and gotchas to watch for:**

- **Replying to a message request without meaning to open the door.** Messenger's official safety page says that if you reply to a message request, you are connected with the sender and any content they sent you is revealed. You do not have to reply. You can leave it in requests, delete it, block the person, or report it.
- **A login or "security" scare, or a forgotten PIN on a new phone.** A message that demands your PIN, password, or a one-time code is a warning sign, not a reason to hurry. Use the in-app Security alerts and your own device, not a link in a chat. And before you switch phones, make sure you actually know your Messenger PIN (or have another official restore method), because Messenger cannot recover the backup for you.

### 3. Parent / Adult Coach Insight

Read this layer with, or just after, the family overview. It is here to help adults support younger learners without turning the evening into a lecture, and without pretending the apps can parent for you.

#### How Family Center and Teen Accounts actually work

**Instagram Teen Accounts** are Instagram accounts for teens ages 13–17 that are automatically given more protective settings. Parents do not have to approve a teen's use of Instagram for these defaults to apply. The built-in limits include a private account (with the age rules in Section 2), tighter messaging, tighter tags and mentions, the strictest Hidden Words setting, sleep mode from 10 PM to 7 AM, and a 60-minute daily reminder. Teens under 16 need a parent's or guardian's permission to make these settings less protective. Teens 16–17 can change many of them themselves, unless a parent or guardian is already supervising the account. Any teen in supervision needs a parent's approval to make settings less protective.

**Supervision** is a separate, optional step. Meta's Help Center describes it as a set of tools and insights for parents and guardians of teens ages 13–17 (or 14–17 in some regions) across Facebook, Instagram, Messenger, and other Meta apps. Both the parent and the teen must agree. Either person can remove it at any time, and the other person is notified. Only one parent or guardian can supervise a teen's Meta Account. The parent must be over 18. Invites expire after 7 days.

**Family Center** is the place to view and manage the supervised profiles in one dashboard, without jumping from app to app. Parents can open it from **Accounts Center** or at [https://familycenter.meta.com/supervision](https://familycenter.meta.com/supervision). Meta's setup steps are: visit that page, tap **Get started**, select the profiles (or share a link if you cannot find the teen's profile), and tap **Send invite**. Family Center's own help says parents cannot read private message content or chat history. They can see insights such as time spent and contact lists, and — on Instagram — accounts the teen has chats with, not the words in those chats. They also cannot post for the teen or reset the teen's password. They can help with time limits, sleep mode, and some privacy and safety settings, and they can approve or deny a request to make settings less protective.

That balance matters. Supervision is a seatbelt and a dashboard, not a one-way window into a teen's private conversations. Say that out loud to the teen. It builds trust, and it matches what the official tools actually do.

#### Why settings drift after updates

These apps change. Official pages opened for this pack already show several moving parts:

- Facebook and Instagram are "gradually updating people to Meta Accounts," so you may see **Accounts Center** or **Meta Account** where you used to see an older settings menu.
- Some Messenger encryption and PIN tools are "gradually being introduced and may not be available to you yet."
- Some Teen Account limits (for example, live-broadcast restrictions) are still being rolled out.
- WhatsApp is upgrading the two-step-verification PIN to a password.
- A menu that lived in one place last year may have a new label after an app update.

A checkup you did last spring can be out of date without anyone in the family making a bad choice. That is not a reason to panic, and it is not a reason to lecture. It is a reason to walk through the same short list a few times a year.

#### A 10-minute seasonal checkup, without a lecture

Pick a quiet moment — after dinner, on a Sunday afternoon, at the change of a season. Sit on the same couch. Work on one device at a time, with the person who uses that device holding the phone. Your job is to ask useful questions, not to grab the phone and "fix it."

Try questions like:

- "Who do you want to be able to message you?"
- "If a message said your account would be locked in 10 minutes unless you clicked, what would we do?"
- "Where do we keep the backup codes or the WhatsApp / Messenger PIN so we can find them when we need them?"

Praise is not required, and guesswork is not helpful. If you do not know what a setting does, open the official Help Center page together and read the first paragraph. Ten minutes is enough to do Privacy Checkup or WhatsApp's Privacy Checkup, confirm two-factor or two-step is on, and write down one family rule. Stop while everyone is still willing to do it again next season.

### 4. Practical family activity — "The 15-Minute Family Settings Checkup"

Hands-on, no real money, one device at a time. An adult should sit with a younger learner. Teens and adults can lead their own device, with the family nearby for questions.

**You will need**

- One phone, tablet, or computer at a time (the one that already has the app signed in).
- A paper and pencil, or a notes app the family already uses, for backup codes, a recovery email reminder, or a Messenger / WhatsApp code — stored somewhere safe, not in a public photo album.
- A timer set to 15 minutes.

**Do not** enter a password, PIN, or recovery code into a chat, email, or a link that arrived as a message. Change settings only inside the app.

**How to run it (one app, one device, 15 minutes)**

1. **Pick the app and the person (1 minute).** Choose Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, or Messenger. The person who uses the account holds the device. Everyone else watches and asks questions; they do not take over the screen.
2. **Lock the Door (4 minutes).** Turn on, or confirm, the extra sign-in step: two-factor authentication on Facebook or Instagram, or two-step verification on WhatsApp. If Messenger offers a message-storage PIN, create one (or confirm you already have one) and write it down in your safe place. If the app offers recovery codes or a recovery email, set those up and store them too. Skip "trust this device" or "save this browser" if other people can use this device.
3. **Check Who Can See and Reach You (6 minutes).** Use the official checkup or settings from Section 2:
   - Facebook: run **Privacy Checkup** (who can see posts and profile details, how people find you, login alerts).
   - Instagram: confirm **Private account** if that is the family's choice; open **Message controls**; review **Hidden Words**.
   - WhatsApp: run **Privacy Checkup**; set **Silence unknown callers** if you want fewer unknown rings; change who can add you to groups if it is still set to **Everyone**.
   - Messenger: open **Message delivery** and choose where requests go; confirm Security alerts you want to receive.
4. **Pause practice (2 minutes).** Read one official warning together, then answer out loud: "If this message arrived tomorrow, what would we do first?" Good first moves are: stop, do not click, do not send money or a password, and check with a family member another way (a phone call you place yourself, or a face-to-face question).
5. **Write three family ground rules (2 minutes).** Keep them short. Examples a family might choose (write your own):
   - We do not send passwords, PINs, or recovery codes to anyone, even if the message looks official.
   - We do not move a chat with a stranger off the app, and we do not send money because a message said it was an emergency, until we confirm it another way.
   - We run this 15-minute checkup again at the start of each season, one device at a time.

**If you have time later the same week**

Repeat the 15 minutes on the next device or the next app. Do not try to finish every account in one sitting. A short checkup that actually happens is better than a long one that gets put off.

**At the end, talk about**

- Which habit felt easiest, and which one surprised you?
- Did any default setting (for example, WhatsApp groups set to **Everyone**, or Instagram message requests) let more people reach you than you wanted?
- Where will the family keep backup codes or PINs, and who is allowed to see them?

### 5. Reflection, common mistakes, and a check for understanding

#### Reflection questions

**For younger learners:** If a message says "Click now or your account will be closed," what is the first thing you do? Who in the family would you show it to before you tap anything?

**For teens:** Which people should be able to message you on each app, and which requests can wait in a request folder? If a parent sets up optional supervision, what should they be able to see (time spent, connections) and what should they not be able to see (the words in your chats)? How will you talk about that together?

**For adults / parents:** Which of your own settings have you not looked at since you first signed up? If an app update moved a menu, how will you find the official Help Center page instead of guessing? Which family ground rule will you actually keep?

#### Three common mistakes (and how to spot or fix them)

1. **"It's private / it's encrypted, so we're safe."** A private Instagram account, WhatsApp's end-to-end encryption, or a Messenger lock on a chat is a strong lock. It does not stop a scam from a person you decided to talk to, a fake "family emergency," or a disappearing message that gets forwarded or saved in a backup. Fix: keep the lock, and still use the Pause habit.
2. **"I'll set up the extra sign-in step later," or "I can always get a new code."** Two-factor or two-step protection only helps if it is on, and if you can still get in when you change phones. Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger all describe backup paths (recovery codes, a recovery email, a PIN). Messenger is especially blunt: it cannot reset your PIN or restore your backup for you. Fix: turn the extra step on today, and store the backup in a place the family can find.
3. **"We already did this once," or "I'll just reply to that request to see what they want."** Settings drift after updates. And on Messenger, replying to a request connects you with the sender and reveals the content they sent. Fix: put the 15-minute seasonal checkup on the family calendar, and treat a new request as a request, not as a conversation you have already accepted.

#### Check for understanding (3 questions)

**Q1.** What do Facebook and Instagram mean by two-factor authentication, and what does WhatsApp call its similar extra sign-in step? Why does this pack nickname it "Lock the Door"?

**Q2.** A 15-year-old wants to change an Instagram Teen Account from private to public. According to Instagram's official Teen Account rules, what is required? What if the same teen is already in parent supervision?

**Q3.** A disappearing WhatsApp message is turned on for 7 days. Which of these is true? (a) No one can ever keep a copy. (b) The setting can still leave a copy in some cases, such as a forward to a chat that does not have disappearing messages on, or a backup made before the message disappeared. (c) WhatsApp staff can read the message because disappearing mode turns encryption off.

**Answers**

**A1.** Two-factor authentication (Facebook and Instagram) and two-step verification (WhatsApp) add an extra check — such as a login code, a password or PIN, a passkey, or a security key — when someone tries to open the account from a new device. This pack calls it "Lock the Door" because it is an extra lock after the password, not a replacement for choosing who you talk to.

**A2.** Teens under 16 need a parent's or guardian's permission to switch the account to public. If supervision is set up, a teen of any age in that supervision needs a parent's approval to make the setting less protective.

**A3.** **(b)** is true. WhatsApp's Help Center says a forwarded disappearing message will not disappear in a chat that has the feature off, and a message can be included in a backup taken before it disappears. Encryption stays on for personal chats; disappearing messages do not mean WhatsApp can read them, and they do not mean every copy is gone.

Claims in this pack were checked against official Help Center and Family Center pages actually opened on 27 Nov 2026 (America/New_York). Menu paths and feature names are taken from those pages. If a screen on your device differs, follow the official page for your device.

**Facebook and Meta account security**

- Facebook Help Center, "Facebook Privacy Checkup" (iPhone app help, opened 27 Nov 2026). Privacy Checkup reviews who sees what you share, account security, how people find you, data settings, and ad preferences; some settings require your main profile; iPhone path through Menu, Settings & privacy, Settings, then Privacy Checkup.  
  https://www.facebook.com/help/iphone-app/443357099140264  
  Canonical Help Center entry: https://www.facebook.com/help/443357099140264
- Facebook Help Center, "How two-factor authentication works on Facebook" (opened 27 Nov 2026). Extra login code or confirmation on an unrecognized browser or device; Accounts Center or Meta Account; methods include a security key, an authentication app, or SMS; recovery login codes; do not save the browser on a public computer.  
  https://www.facebook.com/help/148233965247823
- Meta Help Center, "About Advanced Protection for your Meta Account" (updated about a week before this pack was written; opened 27 Nov 2026). Stronger login methods; Meta will never ask for your password in an email or message; confirm Advanced Protection emails from `security@facebookmail.com`.  
  https://www.meta.com/help/meta-account/1791497342067051/
- Facebook Help Center, "Avoiding scams on Facebook" (opened 27 Nov 2026). Do not respond to a suspected scam; report it; slow down, spot-check, and do not send; do not click links in a suspicious message claiming to be from Facebook; check Facebook settings for official email; common scam types including emergencies, impersonation, and requests to move the conversation off Facebook.  
  https://www.facebook.com/help/1674717642789671
