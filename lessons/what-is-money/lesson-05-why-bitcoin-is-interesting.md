## Family Co-Learning Flow

### 1. Warm kid-friendly explanation

In Lesson 4, Maya's family ran a tiny pretend market. There was **one note keeper**. When that keeper made extra notes, the same lemonade cost more, and Maya's Later jar bought less. We learned a four-check scorecard:

1. **Does it last?**
2. **Can it be divided?**
3. **Is it hard to fake?**
4. **Can people trust the supply and the rules?**

Today's question starts right there: **what if, instead of one note keeper, everyone shared the same notebook and the same public supply rule?**

That question is why some families, and many adults, find **Bitcoin** interesting to study. This lesson is a careful look, not a sales pitch. We are not picking a "best" kind of money. We are practicing the same four checks on a newer form of money, with pretend tokens only.

#### What Bitcoin is, in plain words

Imagine a notebook that records who has how many pretend tokens, and who sent tokens to whom. Now imagine that many people keep a matching copy of that notebook, and they check new lines together.

Bitcoin is a **shared digital notebook** — often called a ledger or a blockchain — that many computers keep and check. When someone sends bitcoin to someone else, the network writes that send into the public record. The 2008 paper that introduced Bitcoin, by Satoshi Nakamoto, describes a way for one person to send electronic cash to another person without going through a financial institution, and without needing one trusted middle party to stop the same coin from being spent twice.

Two ideas matter for our scorecard:

- **There is no single vault keeper.** No one person holds the only copy of the notebook. Many computers share the same record and check that new lines follow the rules.
- **The supply rule is public.** Bitcoin.org and a 2013 Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago explainer both describe a fixed cap: the rules are designed so the total number of bitcoins approaches, and does not go past, **21 million**. New bitcoins are added on a published schedule that slows down over time. One keeper cannot quietly print a stack of extra units the way Lesson 4's note keeper could.

A bitcoin can also be split into very small pieces, so people can make change. Bitcoin.org says each bitcoin can be divided into 100 million smaller units, and into eight decimal places. In this family lesson we will only ever use **pretend** pieces. Kids do not use real bitcoin or real sats here.

Bitcoin still has to pass the same tests as any other kind of money. A public supply cap is not the same thing as a price that stays still. A shared notebook is not the same thing as a magic undo button.

#### Worked example — Maya and the two notebooks

All amounts below are **pretend**. No real money, and no real sats.

Maya's family is back at the tiny market. They have **21 pretend tokens** in the whole game — a small stand-in for a public cap everyone can see. A cup of lemonade is posted at **1 token**.

**Round A — One vault keeper.** Only Uncle Leo has the notebook. Maya pays 1 token for lemonade. Leo writes "Maya sends 1 token to the stand." Then, while no one is looking, Leo adds a new line: "Create 10 extra tokens for the box." Soon the posted price is **2 tokens** a cup. Maya's Later jar still holds 5 tokens, but those tokens now buy less. This is Lesson 4 all over again. One person could change the supply.

**Round B — Everyone shares the notebook.** Now Maya, Leo, and two cousins each have a matching paper notebook. The first page says, in big letters: **Stop at 21 tokens. No extra tokens unless everyone agrees to a new rule.** When Maya pays 1 token, she announces the trade. Everyone writes the same line. If Leo tries to write "Create 10 extra tokens," the others look at their copies and at the cap on page one. They can say, "That line does not match the public rule," and refuse to copy it. To spend a token, Maya must also show her **key card** — a unique paper stamp that only she has. The cousins can see the public notebook, but they cannot move Maya's tokens without her stamp.

Round B is a teaching picture of why people study Bitcoin: a shared record, a public supply rule, and no single vault keeper who can quietly add more. It is not a complete picture of real computers, and it does not mean Maya's 5 tokens will always buy the same number of lemonade cups in dollars. That leads us to the four checks.

#### The four checks, applied to Bitcoin

Use the checks as questions, not as a trophy.

1. **Does it last?** Bitcoin is not a paper note that tears or a coin that rusts. It is a digital record copied across many computers. Bitcoin.org warns, though, that if you permanently lose access to your wallet — for example by losing a recovery phrase or key — those coins stay in the record but cannot be moved again. So the network's notebook can last, and a person's own access can still be lost.

2. **Can it be divided?** Yes. A bitcoin can be split into tiny pieces, down to eight decimal places, so people can make change. In our game we only split pretend tokens, never real ones.

3. **Is it hard to fake?** The network's job is to stop someone from spending the same coin twice. New pages are added with **proof-of-work**: computers spend time and electricity to earn the right to add a block of trades, and other computers can check the work. The Bank of England notes that someone trying to cheat would need more computing power than all the miners put together, which makes the system hard to tamper with — and also expensive to run. "Hard to fake" is not the same as "nothing can ever go wrong."

4. **Can people trust the supply and the rules?** The 21 million cap is written into the public rules that participating computers enforce. That is a real difference from Lesson 4's single note keeper. It is also not a promise that the *price* of a bitcoin, measured in dollars or pounds, will stay still. The Bank of England, Bitcoin.org, and the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis all describe sharp price swings. Trusting a public supply rule is different from trusting that a Later jar will buy the same number of goods next month, if you are measuring in ordinary money.

So Bitcoin is interesting to study because of how its notebook and supply rule work. It is not automatically "sound money," and it is not automatically "unsound money." It is a form of money you can put through the same four checks — and the answers are mixed.

### 2. Parent / Adult Coach Insight

Read this layer with (or just after) the kid explanation. It gives the grown-up vocabulary, the reasons people study Bitcoin, and the main criticisms, with sources you can open yourself. This section is education, not advice about what to buy, hold, or invest in. It does not declare Bitcoin, dollars, gold, or any other form of money the "winner."

#### A careful definition

In the 2008 paper *Bitcoin: A Peer-to-Peer Electronic Cash System*, Satoshi Nakamoto proposed "a purely peer-to-peer version of electronic cash" that would let online payments go "directly from one party to another without going through a financial institution." The paper's core technical problem is **double-spending**: in a digital system, a copy is easy to make, so you need a way to stop the same unit from being spent twice without relying on one trusted third party. The proposed solution is a peer-to-peer network that timestamps transactions by adding them to "an ongoing chain of hash-based proof-of-work."

Bitcoin.org describes the result, from the user's point of view, as a wallet program for sending and receiving bitcoins, and behind the scenes as a shared public ledger called the blockchain, which contains every processed transaction. New coins are created by **mining**, and "no central authority or developer has any power to control or manipulate the system to increase their profits."

The Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago's 2013 primer, by François Velde, puts the design in a single sentence: the protocol is "an elegant solution to the problem of creating a digital currency — i.e., how to regulate its issue, defeat counterfeiting and double-spending, and ensure that it can be conveyed safely — without relying on a single authority."

#### Why people study it

Three features come up again and again. None of them, by itself, settles the scorecard.

**1. A fixed 21 million supply cap in open rules.**  
The Chicago Fed letter walks through the issuance math: the block reward started at 50 bitcoins, and it is halved every 210,000 blocks (about every four years at the target pace of six blocks per hour). "This implies that the total number of bitcoins in existence will approach but never exceed 2 × 50 × 210,000 = 21 million," and "this time path will be independent of the size or computing power of the bitcoin network." Bitcoin.org's FAQ and its halving page state the same cap in plain language: "only 21 million bitcoins will ever be created"; "there will never be more than 21 million bitcoins." New coins entered at 50 per block in 2009 and, after four halvings, the reward listed on Bitcoin.org's halving page (opened 27 Nov 2026) is 3.125 bitcoins per block, heading toward zero around the year 2140. The original paper does not name the figure 21 million; it says that "once a predetermined number of coins have entered circulation, the incentive can transition entirely to transaction fees and be completely inflation free." The 21 million figure comes from the issuance schedule described above.

This is the grown-up version of Lesson 4's check 4. People who find Bitcoin interesting often point to a supply rule that is public, scheduled, and not decided by one vault keeper. A fair reading also notes that a rule can still change if an overwhelming share of users adopted different software (Bitcoin.org's FAQ says changing the protocol "can be very difficult and requires an overwhelming majority of users"). The cap has been a core, widely defended rule; it is not magic. We do not claim that no one could ever try to change it.

**2. A shared public ledger without a single vault keeper.**  
Anyone can inspect the money supply on the blockchain, Bitcoin.org says, because "all information concerning the Bitcoin money supply itself is readily available on the blockchain for anybody to verify." The Bank of England explains the same idea as a distributed ledger, or blockchain: valid transfers are bundled into blocks, each block refers to the one before it, and linking them "makes it very difficult to tamper with the ledger." That public check is the grown-up version of Round B in the worked example.

**3. Sending value across a distance without a bank as the required middle step.**  
The 2008 paper's opening claim is peer-to-peer transfer without a financial institution. Bitcoin.org lists "payment freedom" as an advantage: "It is possible to send and receive bitcoins anywhere in the world at any time." The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, in a 2018 Page One Economics essay, likewise notes that some people see Bitcoin as a way "to transfer money to each other very easily (like sending an email), even across international borders." A fair adult reading should add the limits: many real-world buys still use an exchange or other middle service; taxes and other rules still apply; and "can be sent" is not the same as "is widely accepted at the shop on the corner." The Bank of England, updating its explainer on 13 January 2025, says that in the UK no major high street store accepts cryptocurrency, and that paying this way is generally slower and more expensive than paying in a recognized currency such as sterling.

#### Real tradeoffs and criticisms

A honest parent layer has to hold the other side with equal care. These are the main tradeoffs, tied to sources opened today.

**Sharp price swings.** Bitcoin.org says "the price of a bitcoin can unpredictably increase or decrease over a short period of time" and that Bitcoin "should be treated as a high-risk asset." The Bank of England, using Bitcoin as its example, says that from 2015 to 2024 the pound did not change by more than 10% in one day, while Bitcoin rose by 22% in one day and fell by 26% on another. "Huge changes like these show how volatile cryptoassets are," the Bank writes, and "they are a very risky investment." It also says the value could fall to zero. The Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis, in its October 2022 explainer *Beyond the Hype*, reported a historical episode: between November 2021 and June 2022, the value of all crypto assets dropped by nearly $2 trillion, and "Bitcoin is considered one of the most stable crypto assets yet lost almost 70% of its value during this same period." Those are dated historical figures, not a current price. The 2018 St. Louis Fed essay, which uses money's three jobs, argues that unstable value weakens Bitcoin as a **store of value** (especially when prices are falling) and as a **unit of account** (sellers would have to keep changing prices). That is the key family takeaway: a public cap is not the same as a steady measuring stick or a calm Later jar, if you care about what a unit buys in everyday goods.

**Energy use of proof-of-work mining.** Nakamoto's paper is direct: "In our case, it is CPU time and electricity that is expended." The Chicago Fed primer says a valid addition to the chain must include the solution to a difficult problem that is "costly to find (in terms of computer hardware required, electricity consumed, and time expended)." The Bank of England says the same security that makes the ledger hard to tamper with also makes the system "very expensive to run." Supporters answer, as Bitcoin.org's FAQ does, that other payment systems also use energy, and that mining cost is part of how the network is secured. This lesson does not invent a current global energy total, and it does not pretend the debate is settled. Families can hold both facts: the work costs real electricity, and that cost is also how the shared notebook is protected.

**Irreversible errors and lost keys.** Bitcoin.org's "Some things you need to know" page is blunt: "There is no undo button." "If you permanently lose access to your wallet — for example by losing your recovery phrase — your funds are gone permanently. No one — not developers, miners, wallet providers, or exchanges — can recover funds that you permanently lose from a self-custodied wallet." Payments, once confirmed, "cannot be reversed" and can only be refunded by the person who received them. The U.S. Securities and Exchange Commission's Investor.gov page on crypto assets notes that a wallet stores "private keys" or passcodes, not the coins themselves. The kid-layer "key card" is a picture of that fact. The Bank of England adds a related warning: with no banks or central authority to protect you, "if your 'money' is stolen or mishandled, no one is responsible for helping you get it back."

**Scams and fraud.** Because sends are hard to reverse, scams are a serious practical risk. Bitcoin.org's "Avoid Scams" page, opened today, repeats three foundations: transactions are irreversible, so verify before you send; verify the entire receiving address, not just the first or last characters; and "no legitimate person, business, or support team will ever ask for your seed phrase or private key." It lists common patterns, including fake exchanges, fake support and "recovery" offers, giveaways, impersonation (including AI-generated impersonation), and long-running fake investment relationships. Investor.gov likewise warns that many relationship investment scams involve crypto assets, and that fraudsters may impersonate well-known institutions. This lesson does not use unverified loss totals. The family rule is simple: slow down, and never treat an urgent request for a key, a password, or a payment as a lesson activity.

**Evolving rules and official status.** Bitcoin.org says Bitcoin "is not an official currency," that tax and other legal rules still apply, and that "regulations differ significantly from country to country and continue to evolve." The same FAQ says some places restrict or ban use, others license exchanges, and many have been building frameworks for how this technology meets the formal financial system. The Bank of England and, as the St. Louis Fed noted in 2022, a growing number of financial regulators, prefer the word **cryptoassets** (or crypto assets) rather than "cryptocurrencies," in part because they do not treat these assets as a regular form of money. The Institute does not take a policy side. Families should know that the legal picture is not finished, and that it is not the same in every country.

### 4. Practical family activity — The Shared Notebook Game

**Time:** 15–20 minutes.  
**You need:** two or more paper notebooks (or stacked sheets), pencils, a few dozen buttons, scraps, or paper chits as **pretend tokens only**, and one unique "key card" for each player (a playing card, a stamped slip, or a doodle only that person can copy). No real money, and no real sats.

**The setup.** Write a public rule on a cover sheet: "The game starts with 21 tokens in all. No one may create extra tokens unless everyone at the table agrees to change the rule." Put 21 pretend tokens in a bowl. Give each person a key card. Pick a simple "shop" (a drawing of lemonade or a snack) and a starting price of 1 token.

**Part A — One vault keeper (about 5 minutes).** Choose one adult or older learner to be the only notebook keeper. Play two or three trades. Then, without a group vote, the keeper quietly adds 10 extra tokens to the bowl and raises the shop price to 2. Pause. Ask: Who noticed? What can a Later jar of 5 tokens buy now? How does this feel like Lesson 4?

**Part B — Shared notebooks (about 8–10 minutes).** Now every person gets a matching notebook. Copy the starting balances and the "stop at 21" rule onto every first page. Each trade must be announced out loud, written as the same line in every notebook, and marked with the sender's key card. After two honest trades, try these three tests (stay in pretend tokens):

1. **The extra-token test.** Someone tries to write "Create 10 extra tokens." Others check the cover rule and their own copies. Do they accept the line?
2. **The double-spend test.** Someone who just spent a token tries to spend that same token again. Can the matching notebooks catch it?
3. **The lost-key test.** One player turns their key card face down and "loses" it. Can anyone else honestly move that player's tokens? What happens to tokens that cannot be moved?

If you want a light picture of proof-of-work, add one optional step: before a page of trades is "locked," a player must finish a short, checkable puzzle (for example, find a word in a short list, or roll a dice until they get a 6). Others should be able to see that the puzzle was done. Keep it brief. The point is "adding a page takes some work, and other people can check the work," not a real mining lesson.

**Close the game (2–3 minutes).** Ask the three reflection questions above. End by repeating the house rule of this pack: pretend tokens only, no real money, no real sats, and no buying or selling in real life as part of the activity.

## Appendix

### Learning goals

- Connect Lesson 4's note-keeper problem to a new question: a shared notebook and a public supply rule.
- Describe Bitcoin in plain language as a shared digital ledger checked by many computers, with a public 21 million supply cap in its rules.
- Run Bitcoin through Lesson 4's four-check scorecard without turning the checks into a sales pitch or a dismissal.
- Separate a public supply rule from a stable price, and a shared ledger from an undo button.
- Practice, with pretend tokens only, how a family can compare one vault keeper with a matching-notebook rule.

### Three common mistakes (and how to spot them)

1. **Treating the scorecard as a buy signal or a warning to reject Bitcoin on the spot.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says "it passes the cap check, so it is the best money," or "the price moves, so it is worthless."  
   *Redirect:* the four checks are questions, not a team to join. Bitcoin can look strong on the public supply rule and still be a hard everyday measuring stick because of price swings. Hold both facts. This lesson does not tell anyone what to buy or avoid as an investment.

2. **Confusing a public supply cap with a price that stays still.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says the Later jar is "safe" because "there can only be 21 million," while talking about what those units buy in dollars or at the shop.  
   *Redirect:* check 4 is about whether people can trust the *supply rule*. The Bank of England and the St. Louis Fed describe large price swings. A cap on how many units exist is not the same as a calm unit of account or a short-term store of value.

3. **Thinking a lost key, a typo, or a rushed send can be undone by a helper, a company, or a "support" message.**  
   *Spot it when:* a learner says "someone can reset it," or an urgent message asks for a recovery phrase, a key, or a payment to "fix" an account.  
   *Redirect:* Bitcoin.org says there is no undo button, and that no legitimate support team will ask for a seed phrase or private key. In the family game, a lost key card means those pretend tokens stay stuck. In real life, that risk is one reason this pack uses pretend tokens only.

### Check for understanding (3 questions)

**Q1.** In your own words, what is Bitcoin, and how is its notebook different from Lesson 4's single note keeper?  
**Q2.** Maya has 5 pretend tokens under a public cap of 21. The cap does not change, but the dollar price of one token jumps. Which scorecard check is the cap mostly about, and which money job is the jumping price making harder?  
**Q3.** Why does "there is no undo button" matter if someone loses a key or sends tokens to the wrong place?

**Answers**

**A1.** Bitcoin is a shared digital notebook (a public ledger) that many computers keep and check, with a public supply rule that caps the number of bitcoins at 21 million. Unlike Lesson 4's single note keeper, no one person holds the only copy or can quietly add extra units; other people can check the same record and the same rule.

**A2.** The cap is mostly check 4: trusting the supply and the rules. The jumping dollar price makes the "save for later" job (store of value) and the "compare prices" job (unit of account) harder, even if the cap itself did not change.

**A3.** Confirmed Bitcoin sends cannot be reversed by a central helper, and if a recovery phrase or key is permanently lost, those coins cannot be moved again. That is why families should treat keys as serious, ignore anyone who asks for them, and keep this lesson in pretend tokens only.

### Steelman for and against this lesson's framing

**Steelman for.** Lesson 5 does the job Lesson 4 promised: it applies the same four checks to a newer form of money, as a question to examine, not as a sales pitch. The kid layer starts from a story the family already knows (one note keeper adding extra notes) and offers a concrete picture (shared notebooks, a public cap, a key card) that younger learners can act out with pretend tokens. The parent layer steels both sides with checkable sources: the 2008 Nakamoto paper, Bitcoin.org's own documentation, a Federal Reserve Bank of Chicago primer for the 21 million math, Bank of England and St. Louis Fed explainers for volatility and the limits of everyday use, and Bitcoin.org plus Investor.gov for lost keys and scams. It refuses to invent a current price, a current energy total, or an investment conclusion. That is a fair 501(c)(3) foundation for Lesson 6.

**Steelman against.** Any lesson that explains Bitcoin carefully can still be heard as a nudge, especially in a family that already has strong feelings for or against it. A short pack cannot cover software forks, custody choices, how exchanges work, environmental research in full, or the live legal picture in every country. The shared-notebook game is simpler than real proof-of-work, and a paper "cap of 21" can make the rule look more unchangeable than a software ecosystem really is. A strong adult guide should say those limits out loud, keep kids in pretend tokens, and leave any personal financial decision completely outside the lesson.

### Coherence notes (how this builds on Lesson 4 and leads into Lesson 6)

- **Lesson 1 — What money is.** Same three jobs: pay, compare, and save for later. This lesson asks whether a newer digital form does those jobs well, without assuming the answer.
- **Lesson 2 — Cash flow.** The Later jar is still the picture. This lesson adds a new warning: the number of tokens in the jar is not the same as what the jar can buy if prices move.
- **Lesson 3 — History of money.** Bitcoin is a new chapter in the same story: people keep inventing ways to record and transfer value. It is not the end of the story.
- **Lesson 4 — Sound vs unsound money.** This lesson is the direct sequel. The four checks come over unchanged. The new question is the one Lesson 4 left open: what if there is no single note keeper, and the supply rule is public?
- **Lesson 6 — Family applications.** Still ahead. The scorecard, the Later jar, and the habit of checking claims against sources are meant to travel into everyday saving, spending, and sharing, still without a sales pitch.

**Teaching simplifications (not protocol or market claims)**

- The Shared Notebook Game is a family model. It does not fully describe mining difficulty, block intervals, fees, custody tools, software forks, or how exchanges work.
- The paper cap of 21 pretend tokens stands in for the public 21 million rule. The Chicago Fed's phrasing is that the real supply "will approach but never exceed" 21 million. This pack does not state a live circulating-supply number.
- "No single vault keeper can quietly add extra units" describes how participating computers enforce today's rules. Bitcoin.org notes that changing the protocol would require an overwhelming majority of users. This pack does not say a rule change is impossible, and it does not say one is likely.
- "Borderless" here means the protocol can send value to a person far away without a bank as the required middle step. It does not mean laws, taxes, or exchange services disappear, or that every shop accepts bitcoin.
- There is no investment advice in this pack, and no current bitcoin price is used as a teaching number.
